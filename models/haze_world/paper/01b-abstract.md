Can life regulate its planet's climate when that climate responds slowly? We test this in a model inspired by the hazy early Earth. Two microbial groups compete for hydrogen: methanogens make methane, which forms a cooling organic haze, and acetogens divert hydrogen into acetate. Temperature decides which group grows faster, so their competition can hold temperature exactly constant as hydrogen supply changes. That perfect control is fragile. Of 42 exactly holding states, six were unstable outright; the rest first lost stability at climate response times of about seven years or less. Two changes, acetate slowing its own producers and habitats differing in temperature, made control slightly imperfect and far more durable. On four tested branches, a local sensitivity of 0.07–0.32 K per e-fold of supply bought tolerance of a 30-year response, and 1–2 K bought centuries. In invasion simulations, arrival order decided which group survived.

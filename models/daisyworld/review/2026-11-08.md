## What is supported

The implemented growth, area competition, planetary energy balance and
linear local-temperature relation match the source equations and Fig. 1
legend, including the flux conversion to 917 W m⁻², q′ = 20 K and the 273
temperature offset. The source explicitly uses the linear local-temperature
approximation for these figures; substituting the fourth-power local relation
would change the reconstruction target.

The cloud interpretation is supported by the source's requirement that black
daisies remain locally warmer despite their cloud cover cooling the planet,
and by the Fig. 2 comparison. Keep cloud reflectivity in the planetary
balance and the black surface albedo in the local-temperature contrast. The
figure fit supports this interpretation; it does not uniquely establish a
physical cloud model.

All **12 existing tests passed** under Python 3.14.4. Re-rendering the exact
SHA-256-identified scan reproduced every row of the committed fidelity CSV.
These are reproducibility checks, separate from the mathematical and
negative-control checks below.

## Fig. 1a: the equilibrium discrepancy is decisive, its cause is open

Neutral daisies and bare ground both have albedo 0.5, so A = 0.5 regardless of
coverage and their local temperature equals the lifeless temperature:

\[
T(L)=\left[917L(1-0.5)/(5.67\times10^{-8})\right]^{1/4}-273.
\]

The scalar population equation is a logistic equation with temperature-
dependent coefficients. Its positive equilibrium is

\[
a^*(L)=1-\gamma/\beta(T(L)),\quad\beta(T(L))>\gamma;
\]

otherwise the only nonnegative equilibrium is zero. The temperature endpoints
and the resulting positive-equilibrium luminosity window are

\[
T=22.5\pm\sqrt{(1-0.3)/0.003265},
\quad 0.769466<L<1.144162.
\]

Coverage peaks at 0.7 when L = 0.942917. The numerical up-sweep agrees with
this analytic area curve to 3.2 × 10⁻⁹. At L = 1.218, T = 42.03 °C and β = 0:
any extant daisies must decay, with da/dt = −0.3a. The printed tail near that
luminosity cannot represent a positive equilibrium of the stated equations.

A transient from incomplete settling is a possible explanation, but the
previous numerical experiments do not identify the historical procedure.
An unstated model/procedure or an original coding/plotting error also remains
possible. Replace the working attribution to a less-converged run with an
unresolved cause. Preserve the equation-faithful equilibrium rather than
tuning it to the printed hump.

Targeted literature searches used combinations of Daisyworld, neutral
daisies, Fig. 1a, discrepancy, error, erratum and the original title. Inspection
of the original-model/numerical-implementation and altered-feedback sections
of Wood et al. (2008),
[10.1029/2006RG000217](https://doi.org/10.1029/2006RG000217),
found no discussion of this specific discrepancy. This is a negative result
from a bounded search, not evidence that nobody has noticed it. The review's
discussion of numerical pitfalls and clouds is useful context; it does not
resolve the historical anomaly.

## Repair: a population can be lost before it becomes an invader

At L = 1.224 on the two-species **downward** sweep, the solver reports
convergence at (a_w, a_b) = (0.5969455, 0). Yet a rare black daisy has
per-capita growth xβ_b − γ = **+0.101312** in that returned environment.
Re-seeding black at the same luminosity reaches coexistence at approximately
(0.5926603, 0.0806758).

The stopping criterion divides by max(a, 10⁻⁴), so it is not strictly a
per-capita test for tiny populations. Black can decay during establishment of
white, become numerically negligible, and then be removed even though it
could grow in the final environment. The returned state is an equilibrium on
the black-free boundary, but it is unstable to black invasion. This is one
point on a sweep the original Fig. 1d did not plot, and does not change the
reported Fig. 1a or main published-panel findings.

Recommended acceptance condition: when a nominally converged state removes
an enabled species, check that species' invasion rate in the returned
environment. If positive, re-seed and integrate at the same L. Add a targeted
regression for this point. Do not require all possible states to be globally
stable: the source's finite-seed/hysteresis protocol remains part of the
target.

## Fidelity: nearest ink needs a negative control

The original score measures distance to *any* dark pixel. Axes, ticks, labels
and a different curve can all supply a match. An all-zero population earns a
median distance of 0 px and **100% within 3 px** in panels 1b, 1c, 1d and 2
because it follows the area-zero axis. A zero median alone therefore cannot
establish quantitative reproduction of a particular population curve.

An extra check excludes model area ≤5%, to isolate the substantial occupied
branches. It is still a one-way, nearest-ink score, not digitisation of an

decorative ink and measure agreement in both directions; keep jump locations
as a separate comparison. No new historical causal explanation follows from
the current score.

## Sweep transitions are not all mathematical bifurcations

The paper's 0.01 reseed is a finite perturbation. It can cross a basin boundary
before the lifeless equilibrium loses stability. For black daisies, the
infinitesimal invasion boundary is L = 0.716118, whereas the 0.01-seeded
0.002-grid sweep establishes at L = 0.714. Changing the seed from 10⁻⁴ to
0.1 moves establishment from 0.718 to 0.704. Keep 0.01 for fidelity, but call
the tabulated jumps *transitions under the reseeding protocol*, unless a
separate stability calculation identifies a bifurcation.

Similarly, the published offsets in the sweep direction are consistent with
finite settling; their sign alone does not identify that explanation. Scan
resolution, luminosity step and finite reseeding also limit the inference.

Whether life can regulate planetary climate is a long-standing question, yet biotic thermostats are usually analysed at equilibrium. We ask whether such regulation survives the slow response of climate, and what robustness costs. In a conditional model of a hazy Archean Earth, acetogens and methanogens compete for hydrogen, and methane cools the planet through organic haze. With fixed growth thresholds, competitive exclusion acts as integral feedback, holding temperature exactly against hydrogen supply, as earlier static models found. That exactness is fragile: every exactly regulating state lost stability at climate lags of seven years or less. Leaky regulation, through acetate self-limitation or spread in habitat temperature, was robust: conceding 0.07–0.32 K per e-fold of supply bought a 30-year margin, and 1–2 K bought centuries. Under finite populations, invasion order decided which guild persisted. Lasting biotic regulators must be leaky, and which one a planet acquires depends on its history.
